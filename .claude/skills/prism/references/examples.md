# Worked Examples

Five messy requests, each refracted through PRISM. Use these to calibrate: notice how the recommendation follows from the *inferred outcome*, not the requested format. Assumptions shown are illustrative — in a real run they'd be confirmed or explicitly labelled.

---

## Example 1 — "Create a deck about AI for leadership."

- **Inferred true objective (likely):** get senior leadership to recognise that AI needs near-term operating attention and to **decide whether to sponsor a bounded AI adoption pilot** — not to be generally educated about AI.
- **Mode:** decision support (with a thin education layer).
- **Audience current state:** AI feels important-but-abstract; no owner; "we'll get to it"; unsure if it's hype or urgent.
- **Desired future state:** convinced there is a concrete, bounded, low-risk first step worth funding now, and willing to name a sponsor.
- **Recommended artifact:** **Decision memo (primary)** — the ask is a sponsorship/funding decision. **Presentation outline or Deck IR (secondary)** for the meeting where it's discussed.
- **Why not default to PowerPoint:** a deck optimises for *understanding*, but the blocker is *decision + ownership*. A deck about AI would inform the room and change nothing. The memo forces a specific, bounded ask and pre-empts the "too risky / too early / who owns it" objections.

## Example 2 — "Prepare an update about our cloud migration for senior stakeholders."

- **Inferred true objective:** depends on one question — is a decision needed, or just awareness? If the migration is on track → keep stakeholders confident and unsurprised. If it's off track or needs more budget/time → get a specific decision.
- **Mode:** education/alignment (on track) *or* decision support / escalation (off track).
- **Current state:** stakeholders assume it's fine / have lost the thread / are nervous about cost overrun.
- **Desired future state:** either "confident and informed, no action needed," or "aligned on a specific corrective decision."
- **Recommended artifact:** **Executive one-pager** if it's a status/confidence update; **Decision memo** if money, scope, or timeline needs a call. Clarify this first — it flips the artifact.
- **Why not default to PowerPoint:** "an update" usually means a one-page status, not a slide marathon. If a decision is hiding inside the "update," a deck would obscure it.

## Example 3 — "Explain why we should move away from the current workflow engine architecture."

- **Inferred true objective:** get the relevant technical and/or budget owners to **agree to replace/re-platform the workflow engine** (or at least fund a spike) — persuasion backed by technical reasoning.
- **Mode:** decision support built on a technical briefing; persuasion undertone.
- **Current state:** the current engine "works"; status-quo bias is strong; migration is seen as costly and risky with unclear payoff.
- **Desired future state:** decision-makers accept that the current architecture's costs/risks now outweigh migration cost, and approve a next step.
- **Recommended artifact:** **Technical briefing** if the audience is engineering leadership who must weigh trade-offs; **Decision memo** (with a technical appendix) if the audience is budget owners who need the ask and risk, not the internals. Often both: briefing → memo.
- **Why not default to PowerPoint:** the argument lives in trade-offs and failure modes that slides flatten. A briefing/memo lets the reasoning and the ask both survive scrutiny.

## Example 4 — "Create a presentation about an AI recruitment platform."

- **Inferred true objective:** depends entirely on audience. Investors → get a term sheet / next meeting. Internal execs → fund/prioritise the build. Prospective customers → book a pilot. The *topic* is constant; the *outcome* and artifact are not.
- **Mode:** persuasion (investors/customers) or decision support (internal).
- **Current state:** skeptical of "another AI tool"; unsure of differentiation, ROI, or defensibility.
- **Desired future state:** convinced of a specific wedge and next step (invest / fund / pilot).
- **Recommended artifact:** **Strategy brief** if the need is to establish direction and positioning; **Decision memo** for an internal fund/prioritise call; **investor-style narrative (presentation outline / deck IR)** if pitching for capital. Clarify audience before choosing.
- **Why not default to PowerPoint:** even for an investor pitch, the *narrative and the ask* must be designed before slides. Jumping to slides produces a feature tour, not a fundable story.

## Example 5 — "I need to tell leadership our current delivery plan is risky."

- **Inferred true objective:** make leadership feel the risk they are currently underweighting and **get a decision** — more time, more resource, reduced scope, or accepted risk with sign-off. This is escalation, and escalations that don't end in a decision fail.
- **Mode:** escalation → decision support.
- **Current state:** leadership believes the plan is fine (or hasn't looked closely); the messenger fears looking like they're making excuses.
- **Desired future state:** leadership shares an accurate view of the risk and owns a specific corrective decision.
- **Recommended artifact:** **Executive escalation memo / decision memo** — risk stated plainly up top, quantified impact, options with a recommendation, and an explicit ask + owner. Protects the messenger by being specific and solution-oriented, not alarmist.
- **Why not default to PowerPoint:** escalation needs an unambiguous written record and a clear ask; a deck softens urgency and lets the room defer without deciding.

---

**Pattern across all five:** the format the user named rarely survives contact with the inferred outcome. The recurring primary artifact is a **decision memo**, because most "make me a deck/update/presentation" requests are really "help me get a decision or a shift in belief." Reason from the outcome, then choose.
