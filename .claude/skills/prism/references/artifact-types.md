# Artifact Types

How to choose and defend the seven PRISM artifacts. Choose from the audience outcome, never from the format the user named. Each entry: when to use, when *not* to, typical structure, common failure mode.

---

## Executive one-pager
**Use when** a busy senior audience needs to grasp a situation fast and there is no single binary decision on the table (status, position, orientation).
**Do not use when** a specific approval/funding decision is required (use a decision memo) or the audience needs to be walked through a story live (presentation outline).
**Typical structure** — headline takeaway · situation in 3–4 lines · what it means for them · key numbers · what happens next / what we need.
**Common failure mode** — becomes a dense status report; the "so what" and any ask get lost in detail.

## Decision memo
**Use when** the audience must approve, fund, sponsor, prioritise, choose between options, or endorse. The default for decision support.
**Do not use when** no decision is actually needed yet (you are still educating or discovering) — forcing a premature ask erodes trust.
**Typical structure** — the decision requested (up top) · context / why now · options with trade-offs · recommendation · risks & mitigations · objections pre-empted · the ask (owner, size, timing).
**Common failure mode** — buries the ask on page two; presents options without a recommendation; ignores the objection that actually blocks the yes.

## Strategy brief
**Use when** the audience needs strategic context, direction, or a narrative that orients many decisions over time (not one approval).
**Do not use when** the need is a single fast decision (decision memo) or deep technical understanding (technical briefing).
**Typical structure** — where we are · where we're going and why · the strategic choices · what changes · implications by stakeholder · guardrails / principles.
**Common failure mode** — vision-level platitudes with no implications for action; reads as inspiration, changes no behaviour.

## Technical briefing
**Use when** the audience must understand architecture, implementation, trade-offs, systems, or technical risk well enough to weigh in or decide.
**Do not use when** the audience is non-technical and only needs the outcome and the ask (translate to a decision memo / one-pager instead).
**Typical structure** — problem & current architecture · options with technical trade-offs · risks and failure modes · recommendation · migration/impact · what we need from the reader.
**Common failure mode** — depth without a decision; the reader learns a lot and still doesn't know what to do or approve.

## Workshop plan
**Use when** the goal is co-creation, alignment, conflict resolution, or genuine buy-in that cannot be achieved by broadcast — people must participate to own the outcome.
**Do not use when** you already know the answer and only need approval (that's a decision memo dressed up as inclusion — participants will feel it).
**Typical structure** — objective & desired end state · participants & roles · pre-reads · agenda with timed activities · decisions/outputs to be produced · facilitation notes · follow-ups.
**Common failure mode** — a meeting with no decision rights; lots of discussion, nothing owned or resolved afterwards.

## Presentation outline
**Use when** the message will be delivered live and the value is in narrative, pacing, and facilitation — a room to be carried, questions to be handled in real time.
**Do not use when** the audience will read asynchronously (write a memo) or when slides would just be a memo chopped into bullets.
**Typical structure** — opening hook / stakes · the core argument in 3 acts · evidence beats · the ask · anticipated Q&A · close. Maps to speaker moments, not slides.
**Common failure mode** — a document pretending to be a talk; no arc, no ask, death by bullet.

## Deck IR (vendor-agnostic slide intermediate representation)
**Use when** a slide-based artifact is genuinely and specifically required (the forum expects slides, or visuals carry the argument) — and only then.
**Do not use when** any other artifact would raise outcome probability more cheaply. Slides are the most expensive artifact to make well and the easiest to hide weak thinking inside.
**Typical structure** — per slide: number · role · purpose · audience question answered · objection addressed · core message · evidence · visual intent · speaker notes. Never emit a PPTX file in Phase 1 — emit the IR so the thinking is inspectable before pixels exist.
**Common failure mode** — slideware: pretty templates, no argument, no decision; each slide informs but none moves the audience.

---

## Quick chooser

- Someone must **say yes/no/fund** → decision memo.
- Someone must **get oriented fast** → one-pager.
- Many decisions need **direction** → strategy brief.
- Technical people must **weigh trade-offs** → technical briefing.
- People must **own it together** → workshop plan.
- A room must be **carried live** → presentation outline.
- Slides are **genuinely required** → deck IR.

Primary + secondary is common and encouraged: e.g. *decision memo* (to get the yes) + *presentation outline* (for the meeting where the yes is given).
